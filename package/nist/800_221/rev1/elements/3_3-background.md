The second step in the risk management life cycle involves identifying a comprehensive set of risks, including the risks involved in failing to pursue opportunities, and recording them in the risk register. ICT risk identification is composed of four inputs:

- 1\. Identification of the organization’s mission-supporting assets and their valuation,

- 2\. Determination of potential threats that might jeopardize the security or performance of those assets and potential ICT opportunities that might benefit the organization,

- 3\. Consideration of the vulnerabilities of those assets, and

- 4\. Evaluation of the potential consequences of risk scenarios.

Sec. 3.3.1 through 3.3.4 discuss each of these four inputs in more detail.

Risk practitioners often perform risk identification as both top-down and bottom-up exercises. For example, after the organization has considered critical or mission-essential functions, it may consider various types of issues that could jeopardize those functions as an input to risk scenario development. Subsequently, as a detailed threat and vulnerability assessment occurs, assessors consider how those threats might affect various assets by conducting a bottom-up assessment. This bidirectional approach helps support holistic and comprehensive risk identification.

The risk management processes below should consider both current risk conditions and forward-looking conditions:

- Emerging risks represent those risk types that evolve rapidly enough that existing assessments may quickly become outdated (e.g., expanding risk consideration regarding use of super intelligence, enterprise dependence on a small number of critical cloud service providers).

- Horizon risks are genuinely novel risks for which little historical data exists and that may have long-term future ICTRM implications based on the detection of early, subtle changes in the risk landscape.

Identifying a significant emerging or horizon risk should be treated as a potential trigger for reviewing and updating existing risk appetite and risk tolerance statements (see Sec. 3.2.1) since leadership’s acceptable level of exposure may need to be reconsidered in light of a materially changed threat environment. The ongoing monitoring of emerging risks is addressed further in Sec. 3.7.
