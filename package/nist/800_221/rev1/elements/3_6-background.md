Step 5 of the risk management life cycle is to determine appropriate responses to the risks included and prioritized in the risk register. The practitioner will determine whether the exposure associated with each risk in the register is within acceptable levels based on the potential consequences. If not, that practitioner can identify and select cost-effective risk response options to achieve ICT objectives.

Planning and executing risk responses are iterative activities and should be based on the risk strategy guidance described in Sec. 3.1. Risk oversight authorities monitor the success of those responses and provide operational leaders with financial and mission guidelines to inform future risk management activities. In some cases, risk evaluation may lead to a decision to undertake further analysis to confirm estimates or more closely monitor results (see Sec. 5.1.2). However, risk responses themselves may introduce new risks. For example, adding multi-factor authentication to a business system to reduce an access control risk may introduce a new risk of decreased productivity when users have difficulty authenticating.

While there is some variation among the terms used by risk management frameworks, there are four types of actions (as described in Table 4) for responding to negative ICT risks: accept, transfer, mitigate, and avoid.

In many cases, risk management controls can bring exposure to negative ICT risks within risk tolerance levels. For example, if the risk executive function declares that the organization must avoid risks with high likelihood and impact values for all costs over $500,000, the Risk Response Type column of the risk register (see Fig. 4) can be updated with a response type from Table 4. While including a particular informative reference (e.g., security or privacy controls, Cybersecurity Framework and/or Privacy Framework categories and subcategories) may help guide and describe risk response, additional information will likely be required. In general, people, processes, and technology combine to provide risk management controls that can be applied to achieve an acceptable level of risk. Examples of controls include:

- Preventative: Reduce or eliminate specific instances of a weakness

- Deterrent: Reduce the likelihood of a threat event by dissuading a threat actor

- Detective: Provide warning of a successful or attempted threat event

- Corrective: Reduce exposure by offsetting the impact of consequences after a risk event

- Compensating: Apply one or more controls to adjust for a weakness in another control Consider an organization that identifies several high-exposure negative risks, including poor authentication practices (e.g., weak or reused passwords) that could lead to the disclosure of sensitive customer financial information and the potential for employees of the software provider to gain unauthorized access to and tamper with financial data. The organization can apply several deterrent controls (e.g., warning banners, threat of prosecution for actors who intentionally attempt to gain unauthorized access) and document the applied control identifiers and any applicable notes in the Risk Register Comments column. Preventative controls include applying strong identity management policies and using multi-factor authentication tokens that help reduce authentication vulnerabilities. The software provider has installed detective controls that monitor access logs and alert the organization’s security operations center if internal staff connect to the customer database without a need for access. The financial database is also encrypted so that it protects its data if the file system is exfiltrated. Risk response will often involve creating a risk reserve to avoid or mitigate an identified negative risk or to realize or enhance an identified positive risk. A risk reserve is similar to other types of management reserves in that funding or labor hours are set aside and employed if a risk is triggered to ensure that the opportunity is realized or the threat is avoided. For example, the technical skills needed to recover after an ICT attack may not be available with current staffing resources. A risk reserve can also be used with the accept response type to address this (e.g., by setting aside funds during project planning to employ a qualified third-party to augment the internal incident response and recovery effort).
