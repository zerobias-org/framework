Numerous factors (e.g., financial loss, enterprise reputation, shareholder sentiment) influence priority and should be included in the enterprise risk strategy. An ICT risk that directly impacts the mission is likely to be a high priority, but many other considerations — such as agency or corporate reputation — may move a particular type of risk to the top of the list. Another consideration could occur if a corporate entity were preparing for a merger. Recent examples that demonstrate the discovery of an ICT risk can affect the valuation of an enterprise and subsequent negotiations. There may also be factors that are not directly related to risk but that could support organizational improvement (e.g., quick wins that build team confidence and gain momentum, risks related to an objective that leaders have established as a key priority). Priority values such as low, moderate, and high are often used as risk prioritization categories. This qualitative approach may be more limiting than quantitative analysis in that it is easier to sort a range of numerical values — even those that are relatively close — than it is to sort a list of risks marked “Very High.” In most enterprises, risk strategy should provide direction for both generalization (e.g., low, moderate, high) and more specific risk prioritization methods.
