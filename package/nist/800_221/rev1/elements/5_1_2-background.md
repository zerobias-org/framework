A key purpose of the various risk registers is to enable the ongoing monitoring of enterprise risk activities so that risk managers throughout the enterprise can be informed about the objectives, results, priorities, and opportunities identified through the risk responses. Much of that monitoring occurs by analyzing performance metrics, including those that indicate changes in risk. KRIs inform organizations whether controls are adequately addressing risk and whether risks are changing over time. When KRIs fall outside of pre-established thresholds, risk response is beyond acceptable levels. In this case, organizations should evaluate risks and make any necessary adjustments to the controls. Results of risk activities and decisions are recorded in the risk register.

A key purpose of the various risk registers and reporting methods is to make adequate governance information available to monitor enterprise risk decisions. Enterprise processes should ensure that the communication of risk has been accepted, which implicitly accepts the risk itself (e.g., through an exception process).

Risk activities may also be informed by relevant internal and external audit findings. Significant audit findings often have enterprise-level impacts. However, lower-severity findings may spread through multiple systems to create risk in aggregate if they are not addressed adequately. The coordination of audit findings may span multiple levels of the enterprise. For example, as operational teams address shortcomings or system deficiencies at the system level, key findings might be communicated and tracked by an audit committee. As responses to findings occur and are documented (e.g., through a corrective action plan), they assist in the planning of the subsequent ERM. For some enterprises, the composite set of system risks, responses applied, and other relevant artifacts will be reviewed by a senior official to confirm that risk decisions and risk responses align with risk tolerance and risk appetite directives. Subsequently, risk registers for various risk management disciplines from throughout the organization level are normalized and aggregated to provide a composite view of the risk posture and decisions for that organization. As Level 2 and 3 managers consider feedback from system-level risk activities, they may decide to refine risk tolerance levels. For example, the aggregate risk across multiple systems may represent too great an exposure and need to be reduced. In other cases, based on successful risk management results, stakeholders may permit more risk in some areas to support mission objectives, save resources, or redirect those resources to other areas. Similar reviews and refinement occur at Level 1 to support enterprise governance and risk management decisions. Some types of enterprises may be required to formally disclose risk factors (e.g., through annual reports), and an aggregated understanding of ICT risks and risk decisions can support their fiduciary responsibilities. These activities may also help others (e.g., federal agencies) comply with mandatory requirements, such as those established by OMB.

At Level 3, practitioners interpret the risk tolerance, develop requirements, and apply controls to achieve an acceptable level of risk in a cost-effective way. For example, consider a global retail firm in which the system owner of a customer-facing website selects controls that will ensure adherence to availability service levels. In deciding which controls to apply, the system owner collaborates with a security team to consider methods for meeting service-level objectives. The team can contact the local power utility supplier to determine electrical availability history and gather other information regarding the likelihood of a loss of power to the website. This additional information might help the system owner decide whether to invest in a backup generator to ensure sufficient power availability.

Results from previous assessments can be useful for estimating the likelihood of achieving risk goals in the future. The team would then move to the next risk scenario (e.g., an internet service outage) and review the history and reliability of the organization’s telecommunications provider to ascertain the likelihood and impact of a loss of service. This, in turn, helps risk practitioners demonstrate how their actions directly support mission objectives and enterprise success.
