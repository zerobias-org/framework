Risk registers are used within organizations to communicate and track ICT risks over time. By combining the results of Sec. 3.3.1 through 3.3.4, a practitioner can create a set of risk scenarios to present detailed risk information in context. A complete risk scenario describes the risk source, predisposing conditions, resources affected, and anticipated results.

For ICT risks, a scenario might include a threat source, a threat event, a vulnerability that the threat source might exploit, enterprise assets impacted by the threat, and the resulting harmful impact. For example, “Construction activity severs a critical fiber optic cable that was not protected in conduit, interrupting communications to the data center and resulting in the loss of availability of enterprise financial systems.” Scenarios may also help to describe positive risk (i.e., opportunity). An example of this might be, “Construction of a new alternate data center improves the resilience of financial infrastructure and reduces the likelihood of an interruption.” The list of risk scenarios may include emerging or horizon risks, as described in the introduction to Sec. 3.3.

Organizations will need to determine which assessments should be reflected in the risk register. Some organizations may wish to include both the current risk assessment (before risk response is applied) and the anticipated changes to risk that are expected to result based on the risk response. Risk managers should pay particular attention to workforce considerations, including risk ownership and assignments of risk actions. NIST SP 1308, CSF 2.0: Cybersecurity, ERM and Workforce Management Quick-Start Guide [SP1308], instructs risk management personnel to review the risk owners and risk action owners in risk registers to understand the most impactful risks and to assess internal and external workforce gaps relative to those risks. A risk register for workforce analysis helps leaders understand who should continuously monitor risk conditions and who will be accountable to internal and external authorities. A gap analysis between the assigned risk owner and the risk work role can show whether the practitioner has the competencies necessary to address the problem. Since risk conditions may change as information is aggregated, responsibility and accountability should be periodically reviewed to ensure that the risk owner is appropriate.

Regardless of which model is selected for use as a risk register, it is important for the enterprise to ensure that the model is used consistently and iteratively. As the risk professional progresses through the steps in this section, the risk register will be populated with relevant information. Once decisions have been made as part of a subsequent review of the risks, the agreed-upon risk response becomes the current state after mitigations are put in place, and the cycle begins anew.

Using risk registers for ICT uncertainty provides consistency in capturing, organizing, and communicating risk-related information throughout the ICTRM and ERM processes. The risk registers used at each level convey information about risk assessments, evaluation decisions, responses, and monitoring activities. The remainder of this section provides guidelines and useful information for completing and using registers and integrating them with ERM. It may be necessary to supplement the register with a risk detail record (RDR), which details the considerations, assumptions, and results of risk management activity, including:

- Information regarding the risk itself (e.g., a detailed risk scenario description, threats, underlying vulnerabilities, assets threatened, risk categories, risk assessment results)

- Roles involved in risk decisions and management (e.g., risk owner, risk manager, action owner for specific activities, stakeholders involved in risk response decisions, contractual agreements for supply chain/external partners)

- Schedule considerations (e.g., date the risk was first documented, date of the last risk assessment, completion dates for mitigations, the date of the next assessment)

- Risk response decisions and follow-up (e.g., detailed plans, statuses, risk indicators) An RDR may be stored and maintained in a written record, as part of an organizational knowledge management system, or as a database entry in risk-specific software, such as a Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) application.
