OT risk management should be implemented as a continuous life cycle rather than as a one-time assessment or compliance exercise. In OT environments, risk decisions should account for safety, reliability, environmental, regulatory, and mission impacts and should be revisited as systems, threats, and operating conditions change. Building on the strategy described in Sec. 3.2, this life cycle connects the organizational context and risk management approach established in Sec. 3.1 with the practical activities of framing risk, categorizing systems, selecting and tracking risk responses, applying compensating controls when necessary, planning transitions to more durable solutions, and continuously monitoring whether residual risk remains within defined tolerances.

The OT risk management life cycle should function as an iterative process that reflects OT-specific operational consequences. It links risk framing, system categorization, risk response, transition planning, compensating controls, and continuous monitoring in a manner that supports safe and reliable operations. Compensating controls should be treated as temporary measures rather than permanent substitutes for remediation. Organizations should maintain clear visibility into these temporary conditions and tie them to funded upgrade or remediation plans since adversaries may exploit such gaps when targeting the environment.

Risk management should be guided by the organizational context, roles, responsibilities, and risk tolerances established in Sec. 3.1 and 3.2. These elements define the assumptions, constraints, priorities, and risk tolerance that shape OT cybersecurity decisions. System categorization then translates those priorities into an understanding of system criticality, operational dependencies, and potential consequences.

Once risks are framed and systems are categorized, the organization selects an appropriate risk response consistent with Sec. 3.2.2. Where feasible, risks should be mitigated through engineering changes (e.g., architecture improvements, segmentation, secure configuration, access control, monitoring, patching, replacement of vulnerable components). However, OT environments often include legacy systems, unsupported components, proprietary protocols, fragile integrations, and limited maintenance windows that prevent immediate remediation. In these cases, compensating controls may be used to reduce exposure while maintaining safe and reliable operations.

Compensating controls may not always fully address the associated risk and may need to be treated as temporary mitigations rather than permanent substitutes for correcting the underlying weakness. Examples include additional segmentation, restricted remote access, enhanced logging, manual verification steps, additional jump host controls and monitoring, application allowlisting, increased operator awareness, vendor access supervision, and out-of-band monitoring. Each compensating control should be tied to a specific risk, a residual risk rating, an accountable owner, a review date, and a retirement condition. The risk register described in Sec. 3.2.1 should capture this information.

Transition planning is the mechanism that prevents compensating controls from becoming risk acceptance by default. When the preferred mitigation is not immediately feasible, the organization should establish a funded path to a durable solution. The transition plan should identify the target-state control or architecture, required budget, procurement dependencies, maintenance-window requirements, engineering design work, vendor support, testing and validation activities, implementation milestones, and accountable owner. This ties temporary mitigation decisions to capital planning, outage planning, procurement, and life cycle management.

The OT risk management life cycle operates as a continuous loop: frame risk in terms of mission and operational consequences; categorize systems by criticality and physical impact; select risk responses appropriate to OT constraints; apply compensating controls only when necessary; establish funded transition plans to durable risk reduction; and continuously monitor whether residual risk remains within tolerance.
